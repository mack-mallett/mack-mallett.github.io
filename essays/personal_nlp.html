NLP and Me - How Learning the Tools to Analyze Unstructured Text Data Shaped My Early Career

In many ways, Natural Language Processing (NLP) defined the early stages of my data science career. My first job out of my undergraduate program was as a marketing specialist for a boutique data science consultancy, which at the time specialized in deriving insights from written survey responses using NLP techniques. The addressable market for a firm like this is quite sophisticated, and potential clients expect marketing assets to include some level of detail about how the service works. It was out of this need that I started researching NLP techniques and how to apply them. These clever systems for finding patterns in unstructured data kindled the spark of interest, and I would spend the next five years learning to use these techniques in my own work. The firm I worked for has grown and since pivoted their marketing strategy, but anyone looking for a beginners guide to NLP pipelines can read an archive of my work on the Wayback Machine.

During the five years I spent learning the technical basics of machine learning I also experimented with careers in a variety of business verticals. These experiences shaped my work ethic, and broadened my perspectives on engineering, property development, finance, and government. I learned how to solve business problems; sometimes with technical solutions, but more often with healthy teams, strong relationships, and careful attention to the right details. But, as much as I was learning, I found that I was always searching for opportunities to get back into the world of data science.

One day the opportunity I had been waiting for appeared in my inbox. A team in British Columbia’s Ministry of Citizen Services, which hosts the Office of the Chief Information Officer (Now Connected Services BC), was looking for a data analyst who could analyze unstructured text data, and apply those insights to drive organizational change. It felt like the posting was written for me personally.

This role was transformational for me. The team I joined was genuinely interested in growing the role of data science in the ministry, and using findings as a catalyst to improve the IT service experience of our clients across government. Our main source of fresh insights would come from unstructured text data and our ability to package these insights as effective decision making tools would define our success as a team.

I felt this was the perfect problem for NLP and text analysis techniques. I was extremely lucky, my team members and my boss took a risk on me, and gave me the freedom to experiment and to learn.

The principal NLP technique I used in this role is called topic modelling. Topic modelling is an NLP term for unsupervised clustering techniques applied specifically to text data. This technique allows analysts to segment collections of documents based on semantic similarity. Segments are then reviewed by an analyst, or an LLM, to derive the key themes of that segment. Topic modelling is especially powerful when analyzing thousands or hundreds of thousands of chunks of text, like the kind you get from feedback surveys or technician notes.

One of the most popular topic modelling libraries for Python is BERTopic, a transformer based approach taking advantage of many of the same ideas underpinning modern LLM's. The general pipeline goes something like this:

  1. Chunks of text are vectorized using a transformer. This could be an open source model like BERT, a more specialized model from Hugging Face, or it could be a proprietary model specifically trained on an organization's documents.
  2. Obligatory dimensionality reduction. Natural language in particular can have enormous feature sets, called vocabularies. These vocabularies will invoke the curse of dimensionality if left unchecked.
  3. An unsupervised clustering algorithm. There are many choices for this, but common algorithms include K-Means, density based clustering algorithms, and hierarchical based clustering. BERTopic uses an algorithm called HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise), which allows for clusters of varying densities.
  4. Transforming the clusters back into natural language so they can be interpreted. This involves building a vocabulary for each cluster, and determining which words are the most representative of that cluster.
This project reignited my passion for learning. The pipeline comes as a complete package, but I still wanted to understand the literature behind each step. I found myself comparing alternative clustering techniques, and felt the satisfaction of understanding why getting clusters of varying densities from HDBSCAN matters. I didn't realize it, but I was experienceing the beginning of a transformation from my role as a consumer of models into a designer of data analysis systems.

Fancy models don't mean much if people don't understand what they're saying. For service delivery teams to consider our findings then they would have to be accessible to leaders, at their leisure. In our ministry PowerBI was the common format for this. As a visualization platform PowerBI supports some minor data transformations, but nothing at the scale needed to host a topic modelling algorithm. PowerBI is best when it sits on top of cloud compute (like Azure or AWS), but as a small team we didn’t have access to those resources. To solve this problem, and get our insights on the screens of team leaders, I took on a new challenge of designing and implementing a fully connected data pipeline native to the resources we actually had.

This project became the vehicle for some of the most important technical learning I would do in this early stage of my career. The design had to meet specific requirements, specifically ingesting data from an SQL server, processing unstructured text, computing topics, and serving these topics up to the existing dashboard ecosystem. Since our goal was to use these insights to secure the resources for a cloud based system, the topic modelling pipeline had to be self contained and portable to whatever system we procured. Finally, the system had to be interpretable and easy to run without me, in case I got sick or went on vacation.

To implement my design I would learn a suite of new technologies. SQL and Microsoft SQL Server Management Studio were needed to explore and to programmatically query the tables and views my team was developing concurrently to my work. Since we didn’t have a shared cloud compute resource, the pipeline needed to live as a full Python application which could run on anyone’s standard issue laptop. I implemented object oriented and modular design techniques, and learned new ways to efficiently store massive embeddings as .parquet files.

I practiced my storytelling skills by designing an accessible dashboard for service team leaders, focused entirely on tying their key decision making metrics to the operational level insight of the topics. For added value, the dashboard included some novel visuals describing operational efficiency and inter-topic relationships. Most importantly, I learned how to make use of whatever resources were available to further my team’s stated business goal.

On deployment the project successfully processed over 400,000 pre-existing documents, and more every day after that. The project was novel enough to create a buzz among service delivery leaders. This buzz translated into access to ministry pilots of cloud computing systems, which my team used to transform the way IT data is processed in the ministry and make systems like the one I worked on feasible at scale. The long term effects will entirely depend on how willing service delivery leaders are to leverage data in their decision making process, but I do believe that my team made it as accessible as we possibly could.

NLP techniques gave me the opportunity to transform my love of technology and clever reasoning techniques into a skill set I can build a career around. After the main pipeline and dashboard were deployed and functioning, I started researching how community detection algorithms from graph theory could be used to detect further patterns in the topics found in our dataset. One afternoon, a coworker, who was themselves a student at the University of Victoria, told me that if I was this interested in these techniques I should consider applying to the Masters of Applied Data Science program. This encouragement prompted me to take the next step in my career, where I’ve had the opportunity to learn about so many more fascinating topics in this field.
